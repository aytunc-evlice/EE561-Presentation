Why Cascaded H-Bridge Multilevel Inverters?

Example: three-phase 13-level CHB inverter
Why needed
  • MV loads need 2.3–13.8 kV.
  • Cells in series share the voltage.
  • More levels give a smoother output.
Use when
  • MV drives: pumps, fans, compressors.
  • STATCOM, PV and battery systems.
  • Many levels are needed.
Avoid when
  • Power must flow back to the grid.
  • A common DC link is needed.
  • Voltage is low or few levels are enough.
A three-phase 13-level CHB: six identical H-bridge cells in series in each phase, each with its own isolated DC source E; the three phases are Y-connected and feed the load. Each cell gives +E, 0 or −E, so the phase voltage has 13 levels. Why: medium-voltage loads need kilovolts, cells in series share that voltage, and many small steps give a nearly sinusoidal output. Use it for MV drives of pumps, fans and compressors, and where separate DC sources already exist (PV, batteries) or are not needed (STATCOM). Avoid it when power must flow back: the cells are fed by diode rectifiers, which conduct one way only. Avoid it when one shared DC link is needed, such as HVDC: every CHB cell has its own isolated DC side, so there is no single DC bus; the MMC is used there. For low voltage or three levels, a two-level or NPC inverter is simpler.

Single-Phase H-Bridge Cell

  • Basic building block of the CHB.
  • Outputs +Vd, 0 and −Vd, with two zero states.
  • vAB is shaped by bipolar or unipolar PWM.
S1S2S3S4vAB

Press the rows. +Vd: S1 and S2. −Vd: S3 and S4. Zero from +Vd: S2 turns off and the load current freewheels through S1 and D3. The other zero state: S1 turns off and the current freewheels through S2 and D4. The table shows gate signals: S3 (or S4) gets its complementary gate signal, but it only carries current if the load current reverses. Switches in one leg are complementary, so two gate signals per cell are enough.

Bipolar and Unipolar PWM

fm = 60 Hz, fcr = fsw,dev = 900 Hz (mf = 15), ma = 0.8
    900fsw,inv (Hz)

    A sine reference is compared with a triangular carrier; the carrier frequency is the IGBT switching frequency. Bipolar: S1 and S3 are complementary, vAB is only +Vd or −Vd and the harmonics sit around mf. Unipolar: two references 180° apart, S1 and S3 switch independently, vAB has three levels and the inverter switching frequency doubles while the IGBT frequency is unchanged.

    Unipolar PWM

    Shift the reference or shift the carrier: same result
    S1 on: vm > vcr
    S3 on: −vm > vcr  ⇔  vm < −vcr Same condition, so the gate signals are identical.
    Why the CHB shifts the carrier
    • One reference is shared by all cells.
    • Cells already need shifted carriers (φcr), so the 180° shift fits the same scheme.
    • Simple to implement: only carrier offsets change.
    Top: two references, vm and −vm, against one carrier. Middle: one reference against two carriers 180° apart. Both give the same gate signals, so vAN, vBN and vAB are the same. Each leg pulses at fcr and vAB at 2fcr. In a CHB every cell needs its own carrier phase anyway, so all shifting is done on the carriers and one common reference drives every cell.

    Cascaded H-Bridge Topology – Equal DC Voltages

    Figure 7.3-1  Five-level cascaded H-bridge inverter.
    • Identical H-bridge cells in series in each phase.
    • Each cell has an isolated DC source E and gives +E, 0 or −E.
    • vAN = vH1 + vH2: five levels.

    Isolated DC supplies: multipulse diode rectifiers. If interested, see Wu & Narimani, Ch. 3.

    Fig. 7.3-1: each phase has two H-bridge cells, H1 and H2, connected in series. The bottoms of the three phases join at the neutral N, and A, B and C feed the load. Every cell has its own isolated DC source E, so it can output +E, 0 or −E, and the phase voltage vAN = vH1 + vH2 has five levels.

    Five-Level CHB Topology

    Switching states and redundancy
    • Same level, several states: redundancy.
    • Gives flexibility in switching pattern design.
    m = 2H + 1
    Nsw = 6(m − 1)
    m: number of voltage levels (always odd). H: number of cells per phase. Nsw: number of switches.
    5 levels: H = 2, Nsw = 24 switches.
    vAN = vH1 + vH2. Choose a level, then a row: the drawing shows which switches are on. +E can be made in four ways and 0 in six. The number of levels is always odd, which differs from other multilevel topologies, and the three-phase inverter needs 6(m − 1) switches, 24 for five levels.

    Cascaded H-Bridge Topology – Unequal DC Voltages

    More levels without more cells
    7levels
    3cells per phase
    • Only two cells: 7 levels with E, 2E and 9 levels with E, 3E.
    Three equal cells give seven levels. Two cells with E and 2E also give seven, and E with 3E gives nine, the same as four equal cells.

    Cascaded H-Bridge Topology – Unequal DC Voltages

    Seven levels: equal vs unequal
    • Switching states: 64 with equal DC, 16 with unequal DC.
    • Redundancy is lost: less freedom for switching patterns.
    • Cells are no longer identical: modularity is lost.
    • Result: limited application in industry.
    Number of switching states for each level, counting both zero states of every cell. Equal DC (three cells) has up to 20 ways to make zero; unequal DC (E and 2E) has at most four. Different cells also mean different spare parts and designs.

    Cascaded H-Bridge Inverters

    Advantages and disadvantages
    Advantages
    • ModularIdentical cells; add cells for more voltage.
    • Many levelsLow THD and dv/dt, small filter.
    • Low-voltage devicesEach cell blocks only E.
    • Redundant statesFlexible switching patterns.
    • Fault tolerantA failed cell can be bypassed.
    Disadvantages
    • Isolated DC sourcesBulky phase-shifting transformer.
    • Many components6(m − 1) switches, drivers and sensors.
    • No regenerationDiode-fed cells cannot return power.
    • No common DC linkUnsuited to back-to-back or HVDC.
    The advantages come from building the phase out of identical low-voltage cells: modular, many levels, redundant switching states and cell bypass. The price is the isolated DC supply of every cell, which for a drive means a phase-shifting transformer with diode rectifiers. With diode rectifiers the cells cannot send power back, and there is no common DC link.

    Carrier-Based PWM Schemes – Phase-Shifted PWM

    Seven levels, mf = 3 for clarity, ma = 0.8
    S1k on: vm > vcrk
    S3k on: vm < vcrk−
    φcr = 360°m − 1 = 60°
    vAN = vH1 + vH2 + vH3 S4k and S2k are the complements of S1k and S3k.
    • Inside a cell: unipolar, vcrk and vcrk− 180° apart.
    • Between cells: carriers shifted by φcr.
    • Cells switch at different instants: vAN steps by E only, not the full DC voltage as in a two-level inverter. Lower dv/dt and EMI.
    Pick a cell, or click it in the drawing. Its carrier pair is highlighted: vcrk (solid) gives S1k, vcrk− (dashed, inverted) gives S3k. The lower switches get the complementary signals. Each cell works like the unipolar H-bridge; only the carrier phase differs between cells, by 60° for seven levels.

    Phase-Shifted PWM

    mf = 10, fcr = 600 Hz, ma = 0.8
    24%THD
    7levels

      THD falls from vH1 to vAN to vAB.

      Grey bars show the previous waveform's spectrum. The sideband group around 2mf that each cell has cancels when the three cells add up, so vAN starts at 6mf. In the line voltage the triplen harmonics also cancel, and vAB has 13 levels with a peak of 6E.

      Phase-Shifted PWM

      Switching frequency and output voltage

      Switching frequency

      fsw,inv = 2H · fsw,dev = (m − 1) · fsw,dev Seven levels: fsw,inv = 6 fsw,dev (600 Hz → 3600 Hz)
      • High fsw,inv: harmonics move higher, more are eliminated.
      • Low fsw,dev: low switching losses.

      Output voltage

      Vd = (m − 1)2 E    VAB1,max = √3√2 ma Vd Vd: total DC voltage per phase (H·E). VAB1 is rms.

      Third-harmonic injection: up to 15.5% more output voltage in the linear range.

      Every device switches at fsw,dev, but the phase voltage sees 2H times that, because the cells are interleaved. Third-harmonic injection flattens the reference so ma can rise to 1.155 without overmodulation; the third harmonic cancels in the line voltage.

      Carrier-Based PWM Schemes – Level-Shifted PWM

      Carrier arrangements

      • Same frequency and amplitude, only shifted vertically.
      • IPD gives the best harmonic profile; used from here on.
      Phase-shiftedma = V̂mV̂cr Level-shiftedma = V̂mV̂cr (m − 1) PS: every carrier spans the full range. LS: the m − 1 carriers share it, V̂cr = peak-to-peak of one carrier. Both: ma = 1 at the edge of the linear range.
      IPD: in phase disposition, APOD: alternative phase opposite disposition, POD: phase opposite disposition. The modulation index is defined differently only because the carriers are arranged differently; in both cases ma = 1 gives the same maximum linear output.

      Level-Shifted PWM

      IPD, seven levels, fcr = 1080 Hz (mf = 18), ma = 0.8
      fsw,inv = fcr
      fsw,dev = fcrm − 1 = 180 Hzaverage over all devices
      • Cell outputs are unipolar.
      • Upper band → S1, lower band → S3.
      H1 takes the outer bands, H3 the inner bands. The upper carrier of a pair gives S1 of that cell, the lower one gives S3. Each cell output is 0 or +E in the positive half cycle and 0 or −E in the negative half, so it is unipolar. Counting pulses: the cells switch at 240, 180 and 120 Hz, average 180 Hz = 1080/6.

      Level-Shifted PWM

      Uneven device use and rotation
      • Unequal fsw,dev and conduction time between cells.
      • Fix: rotate the bands among the cells every cycle.
      Without rotation the inner cell conducts 86% of the time and the outer cell 10%, and their devices switch at different rates. Rotating which band each cell uses every fundamental cycle makes all cells equal over three cycles, and the phase voltage does not change.

      Phase-Shifted vs Level-Shifted

      Same average fsw,dev = 600 Hz for a fair comparison
      Phase-shiftedLevel-shifted
      Device switching frequencySame for allDifferent
      Device conduction periodSame for allDifferent
      Rotation of switching patternsNot requiredRequired
      Line voltage THDGoodBetter
      • PS: H1, H2, H3 are the same waveform with a small phase shift.
      • LS: each cell sees a different band, so use is uneven.
      Same average device switching frequency: phase-shifted uses fcr = 600 Hz, level-shifted uses fcr = 3600 Hz. Level-shifted gives lower line-voltage THD, most clearly at low modulation index. Phase-shifted shares the stress equally and needs no rotation.

      Staircase Modulation

      Selective harmonic elimination, seven levels, ma = 0.8
      vAN = Σn = 1,3,5… 4Enπ (cos nθ1 + cos nθ2 + cos nθ3) sin nωt
      cos θ1 + cos θ2 + cos θ3 = 3ma
      cos 5θ1 + cos 5θ2 + cos 5θ3 = 0
      cos 7θ1 + cos 7θ2 + cos 7θ3 = 0
      • H cells give H angles: one sets ma, H − 1 remove harmonics.
      • More levels, more degrees of freedom.
      Each cell gives one pulse per half cycle with its own angle. With three cells we have three equations: one fixes the fundamental, two cancel the 5th and 7th. At ma = 0.8 the angles are 57.1°, 28.7° and 11.5°. Triplen harmonics stay in vAN but cancel in vAB.

      Staircase Modulation

      Looks simple, but has drawbacks
      Why it looks simple
      • Simple to implementEach cell switches once per half cycle.
      • Lookup tableAll angles calculated offline for digital control.
      • Low switching lossesIGBTs switch at the fundamental frequency.
      Drawbacks
      • Low-order harmonics remain11th, 13th, … need a large filter.
      • Poor at low maOnly the inner levels are used.
      • Nonlinear equationsNo solution exists for some ma.
      • Slow dynamicsAngles update once per cycle.
      • Unequal cell powerCells with small θ conduct longest.
      Compared with carrier-based PWM the losses are very low, but the remaining harmonics are close to the fundamental, the waveform degrades at low speed, the angle tables have gaps, and the cells do not share power equally unless the angles are rotated.

      Summary

      • CHB: single-phase H-bridge cells in series on the ac side.
      • Number of cells set by operating voltage, harmonic requirements and cost.
      • Identical cells: modular design, redundant states, low dv/dt and THD.
      • Each cell needs an isolated DC supply: phase-shifting transformer and multipulse diode rectifiers.
      • Unequal DC: more levels, but modularity and redundancy are lost.
      • PS-PWM shares the stress evenly; LS-PWM gives lower THD but needs rotation; staircase has the lowest losses.
      • Widely used in high-power medium-voltage drives.
      The CHB in one slide: identical H-bridge cells in series, each with its own isolated DC supply; equal DC voltages keep the design modular; phase-shifted PWM, level-shifted PWM or staircase modulation, chosen by losses, THD and how evenly the cells must be used.
