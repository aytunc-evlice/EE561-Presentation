Why multilevel?

The switch is the bottleneck

A semiconductor is rated for one blocking voltage. Applications keep raising the DC link.

  • Higher-voltage devices conduct worse and switch slower. The next two slides show why, from the physics and from the losses.
  • Every switching edge of a 2-level leg is a full Vdc step: dv/dt, EMI, motor insulation stress.
Idea: give the leg more than two terminals to choose from, so that each device only ever sees a fraction of Vdc.
Question: one 3.3 kV device — or two 1.2 kV devices plus something that guarantees each one only ever sees half the voltage? That “something” is today’s lecture.

1 min. Click through 800 V → 1500 V → 5 kV. At 1500 V the 2-level leg needs a 3.3 kV class device (≈1 kHz); a 3-level leg gets away with 1200 V parts.

Derating slider: steady blocking is limited (cosmic-ray FIT). Leave it at 67 % — the quiz uses that number. Why a lower class is better: the next two slides.

Why multilevel? · theory · read: Baliga, Fundamentals of Power Semiconductor Devices

Why a lower blocking voltage? The semiconductor view

The off-state voltage is held by the lightly doped drift region. Poisson’s equation, dE/dx = qND/ε, gives a triangular field. Breakdown occurs when the peak reaches the critical field Ec:

VB = ½ EcWD  ⇒  WD = 2VB/Ec,   ND = εEc2/(2qVB)

When the device conducts, the same layer is a series resistor:

Ron,sp = WD/(qμnND) = 4VB2/(εμnEc3) ∝ VB2.5

Ec falls slowly as VB rises (∝ VB−1/6 in Si), which turns VB2 into VB2.5. Doubling the rating costs 22.5 = 5.7× the resistance per area.

Split the voltage over n dies in series with the same total area A:

R = n · Ron,sp(VB/n) / (A/n) = n−1/2 · Ron,sp(VB) / A

Bipolar devices (IGBT, PiN diode) flood the drift region with stored charge. The on-state drop stays low, but that charge, ∝ WD, must be removed at every turn-off. Tail current and reverse recovery grow with the rating.

Question: 4H-SiC has ≈ 10× the critical field of Si. How much lower is its ideal Ron,sp? R ∝ 1/Ec3 gives ≈ 1000×. Lower ε and μn leave several hundred times. The slope is the same, so a lower rating still wins.

2 min. Derive WD and ND from Poisson on the board: the area of the field triangle is the voltage. Then Ron,sp. Press P (or the button) to ramp the blocking voltage: the depletion edge moves as √V and the peak field reaches Ec exactly when the drift region is fully depleted, in both columns at once.

  • Si, ideal (Baliga fits): 1200 V → WD ≈ 101 µm, ND ≈ 1.6·1014 cm−3, Ron,sp ≈ 0.30 Ω·cm². 600 V → 45 µm, 52 mΩ·cm².
  • Two 600 V dies with the same total area: 0.71× the resistance of one 1200 V die. For unipolar devices the multilevel leg is not penalized for putting devices in series.
  • Real devices sit above the ideal line (channel, substrate); superjunction MOSFETs beat it by charge compensation. SiC line: Ec ×10, εr 9.7, μn 950 cm²/Vs → about 1/580 of Si.
  • Bipolar: conductivity modulation removes most of the VB2.5 penalty in conduction, but the stored charge is why 3.3 kV IGBTs switch at about 1 kHz.
Why multilevel? · device losses · simulation, illustrative device data

Why a lower blocking voltage? The loss view

Switching: energy per hard-switched event

Esw ≈ ½ V I (ton + toff) + QrrV
  • A multilevel leg commutates one device against Vdc/(N − 1), not Vdc.
  • The lower class is also faster: thinner drift region, less stored charge → smaller tsw and Qrr.

Conduction

  • MOSFET: n dies at V/n beat one die at V (× n−1/2, previous slide).
  • IGBT and diode: the knee voltages add. Two 1.2 kV IGBTs in series drop more than one 3.3 kV IGBT, so the NPC pays in conduction. T-type and ANPC address this.

And: dv/dt ∝ Vdc/(N − 1); cosmic-ray failure rate rises steeply with V/Vrated; ≤ 1.2 kV opens SiC and GaN, more vendors, lower cost per ampere.

Question: 1500 V link, the 2-level leg runs at 1 kHz. How fast may a 3-level leg switch for the same loss? ≈ 6 kHz with 1.2 kV IGBTs and ≈ 70 kHz with 1.2 kV SiC. Per event at 100 A: 31 mJ and 3.4 mJ instead of 307 mJ.

2 min. Same 1500 V link as the design example. Move the fsw slider: the 2-level leg’s switching loss explodes, while the 3-level legs stay conduction-dominated up to several kHz.

  • Per event at 100 A: 2L, 3.3 kV at 1500 V ≈ 307 mJ; 3L, 1.2 kV IGBT at 750 V ≈ 31 mJ (2× from the voltage step, 5× from the faster class); 3L SiC ≈ 3.4 mJ.
  • Conduction per leg: 2L 155 W vs 3L IGBT 234 W. The NPC pays for two knees in series; that is the honest trade-off.
  • Model: analytic average and RMS currents (unity PF, m = 0.9, Î = 100 A, same integrals as the device-currents slide), E ∝ V·I. Basics · the two-level leg

Starting point: the two-level leg

One complementary pair, S2 = 1 − S1:

vaO = (2S1 − 1) · Vdc/2
  • Two levels: ±Vdc/2 = ±400 V on an 800 V link.
  • The off switch blocks the full Vdc; every edge is an 800 V step.
  • The current sign decides whether a switch or its antiparallel diode conducts.
Question: with ia > 0 and S1 = 0, which device conducts? D2, the antiparallel diode of S2 — figure (b).

Basics · notation used in both parts

Basics: pole, phase and line-to-line voltages

Pole voltage vaO: the leg output measured from the DC midpoint O. An N-level leg has N values.

vab = vaO − vbO   line-to-line, up to 2N − 1 levels
vnO = ⅓ (vaO + vbO + vcO)   common mode
van = vaO − vnO   phase voltage
Anything added equally to all three pole voltages — triplen harmonics, a common offset — cancels in vab and van of a balanced three-wire load. Part II uses this for min-max injection.
m = V1,pk / (Vdc/2)   modulation index
mf = fc / f1   frequency ratio
THD = √(Σh≥2 Vh2) / V1
ΔV = Vdc/(N − 1),   dv/dt ≈ ΔV/tr

800 V link, tr = 50 ns: 2-level → 16 kV/µs, 3-level → 8 kV/µs. A 1 nF parasitic capacitance to ground then carries i = C dv/dt = 16 A vs 8 A spikes (details: EMI/EMC module).

Question: with a 3-level leg per phase, how many distinct values can vab take? Five: 0, ±Vdc/2, ±Vdc (2N − 1 = 5).

2 min. Fix the notation once; both parts use it. The common-mode cancellation is the key fact for the next slides (triplen harmonics) and for min-max injection in Part II. Part II opens with two animated slides on sine-triangle PWM, ma and mf.

Why multilevel? · simulation

More levels, smaller steps

4 min. Start at N = 2 (square wave, THD ≈ 48 %). “What THD do you expect at N = 5?” Then press Sweep.

  • Step ΔV and device voltage fall as Vdc/(N−1).
  • Staircase THD falls roughly as 1/N — with only one switching per level per half-cycle.
  • Switch to PWM: the harmonics move to the carrier band; more levels shrink that band too → smaller filter.

Cost (say it now, we pay it later): 2(N−1) switches, more gate drivers, and capacitors that must stay balanced.

Why multilevel? · theory · read: Rodríguez, Lai, Peng, IEEE TIE 2002

Staircase synthesis: choosing the switching angles

Quarter-wave symmetric staircase: s = (N − 1)/2 steps of height E = Vdc/(N − 1) at angles α1 < … < αs. Only odd harmonics remain:

Vh = (4E / hπ) Σk cos(hαk)

3 levels, one angle: Vh = (4/hπ)(Vdc/2) cos hα. With α = 30°, cos 90° = 0: no 3rd harmonic, THD = 31.1 % — the shape of a 2-level line-to-line voltage. α = 18° removes the 5th instead.

5 levels, two angles: one equation sets the fundamental, one removes the 5th:

cos α1 + cos α2 = πm/2,   cos 5α1 + cos 5α2 = 0

m = 0.8 → α1 = 30.65°, α2 = 66.65° (36° = 180°/5 apart).

Question: in a three-phase converter, why spend an angle on the 5th rather than the 3rd? Triplen harmonics are common mode and cancel in vab; the 5th and 7th do not.

3 min. Selective harmonic elimination: fundamental-frequency switching, used where switching losses dominate (MV, IGCTs). Press the presets: 30° removes h = 3, 18° removes h = 5; the 5-level pair keeps m = 0.8 and removes h = 5. The “More levels” simulation used nearest-level angles: for 3 levels at m = 1 the step sits at arcsin(0.5) = 30° — the same angle.

From two levels to three: the NPC leg

Question 1. From O to P: which switches change? S3 off, then S1 on. Only the pair (S1, S3) commutates; the pair (S2, S4) stays.

Clamp · analysis

NPC in equations: switching functions and averaging

Gate signals S1 … S4 ∈ {0, 1}, complementary pairs S3 = 1 − S1, S4 = 1 − S2:

vaO = (S1 − S4) · Vdc/2
iP = S1ia,   iN = S4ia,   iO = (1 − S1 − S4) ia

Off devices block Vdc/2. For an N-level clamped leg: Vdc/(N − 1).

Apply the local average over one switching period, dP = ⟨S1⟩, dN = ⟨S4⟩:

⟨vaO⟩ = (dP − dN) Vdc/2,   ⟨iO⟩ = (1 − dP − dN) ia

Carrier PWM with reference r: if r > 0 then dP = r, dN = 0 (and the mirror case), so

⟨iO⟩ = (1 − |r|) ia

This one line is the starting point of the balancing analysis in Part II.

Question: single leg, r = m sin θ, ia = I sin θ. Is ⟨iO⟩ zero over a fundamental period? Over each half period? Zero over the period, but not over each half: one leg pumps the midpoint at the fundamental frequency. Three legs cancel down to a ripple at 3f1.

2.5 min. Same derivation style as the Control & Modelling module: switching functions → local average. Write ⟨iO⟩ = (1 − |r|) ia on the board and leave it there for Part II.

Clamp · 3-level T-type (TNPC) · read: Schweizer & Kolar, IEEE TPEL 2013

T-type: one device in the path

Same gate table as the NPC: pairs (S1, S3) and (S2, S4). The midpoint is reached through a bidirectional switch S2–S3.
In P and N only one device conducts (NPC: two) → lower conduction loss.
But S1 and S4 block the full Vdc → 1200 V class at 800 V. Sweet spot: links up to ≈1000 V, tens of kHz.

2.5 min. Switch between NPC and T-type in state P with ia > 0 — count the devices in the path (2 vs 1). Then show state P with S4 blocking 800 V.

Typical homes: UPS, string PV inverters, motor drives ≤ 690 V AC.

Clamp · 3-level active NPC · read: Brückner, Bernet, Güldner, IEEE TIE 2005

ANPC: the clamp gets a choice

Clamp diodes become switches S5, S6: zero can be made through the upper path OU …
… or the lower path OL. Same vaO = 0, different devices carry the current.
Even which switch commutates is a choice: OL with S1 kept on makes S2 switch instead of S1. A free choice needs an objective: losses, temperature, or the midpoint.

2.5 min. Step 1: OU with i>0 → D5 + S2 (exactly the NPC path). Step 2: OL with i>0 → S6 + D3. Step 3: OL+S1 — same path, but S1 stays on, so S2 blocks the 400 V: going P ↔ OL+S1 switches S2, not S1.

Hybrid Si/SiC ANPC modules (e.g. Infineon, 2019, 1500 V PV with 1200 V chips) put fast SiC where the commutation happens and IGBTs where current only conducts.

Device currents in an NPC leg: average and RMS

Unity power factor, r = m sin θ, ia = Î sin θ. In the positive half-cycle S1 conducts in P (duty m sin θ), D5 in O (duty 1 − m sin θ), S2 in both. The negative half mirrors S4, S3, D6.

Iavg,S1 = (1/2π) ∫0π m sin θ · Î sin θ dθ = mÎ/4
Irms,S12 = (1/2π) ∫0π m sin θ · Î2 sin2θ dθ = 2mÎ2/3π
Iavg,S2 = Î/π,   Irms,S22 = Î2/4
Iavg,D5 = Î(1/π − m/4),   Irms,D52 = Î2(1/4 − 2m/3π)
Pcond = V0Iavg + rIrms2,   Psw = fswksw(Vdc/2)Î/π

Who works hardest? Loss distribution

Question 1. NPC, unity PF, m = 0.9: hottest device? Outer S1/S4: they conduct and switch (S1 ↔ D5). S2/S3 conduct but never switch.
Question 2. Same NPC as a rectifier (φ = 180°)? Commutation moves to S3 ↔ D1: the inner switches now conduct and switch — NPC’s worst case.
Question 3. ANPC, same rectifier point. Who switches now? The active clamps S5/S6 take the commutation; the hottest device drops by roughly a third. Same levels, hot spot gone.

Float · 3-level flying capacitor (Meynard & Foch, 1992) · read: Wu & Narimani, Ch. 9

Flying capacitor: carry the level with you

vaO = −Vdc/2 + S1(Vdc − Vfc) + S2Vfc
With Vfc = Vdc/2, both 10 and 01 give 0 V.
Cfc dVfc/dt = (S1 − S2) ia
The two zero states move charge in opposite directions. No clamp diodes, no midpoint needed.
Question: Vfc has sagged to 360 V and ia > 0. Which zero state? 10: the current enters the + terminal and charges Cfc. If ia < 0, use 01.

4.5 min. Capacitors are “real” here: holding a state moves Vfc in slow motion (20 A, 1 mF → 20 V per ms, shown as 20 V/s).

  • With Vfc = 360 V the “zero” level is actually ±40 V: imbalance distorts the output levels and overstresses devices.
  • Rule for the board: choose the redundant state with sign(ia) · (Vfc* − Vfc) > 0.
  • Costs: one capacitor per leg, pre-charge at start-up, Vfc must be regulated. Where: multilevel DC–DC, GaN FCML converters in research.
Float · analysis

Flying capacitor in equations: averaging and phase-shifted carriers

Switching functions S1, S2 (complements S1′, S2′):

vaO = −Vdc/2 + S1(Vdc − Vfc) + S2Vfc
Cfc dVfc/dt = (S1 − S2) ia

Local average over one switching period, with duties d1, d2:

⟨vaO⟩ = −Vdc/2 + d1(Vdc − Vfc) + d2Vfc
⟨ifc⟩ = (d1 − d2) ia

Phase-shifted carriers (180° apart), same reference → d1 = d2 = d:

  • ⟨ifc⟩ = 0: no net charge per switching period.
  • ⟨vaO⟩ = (2d − 1)Vdc/2, independent of Vfc.
  • Each switch runs at fsw; the output ripple is at 2fsw.

Balancing: set d1 − d2 = k·sign(ia)·(Vfc* − Vfc). A small duty difference moves charge while the average output changes only by (d1 − d2)(Vdc/2 − Vfc) ≈ 0.

Question: d1 = d2 = 0.5 but Vfc = 360 V (Vdc = 800 V). What does the output do? ⟨vaO⟩ = −400 + 0.5·440 + 0.5·360 = 0 V, but the zero states now give +40 V and −40 V: more ripple, wrong levels — and nothing pulls Vfc back.

2 min. Same averaging as the NPC slide. Message: phase-shifted PWM is charge-neutral by construction, but it is not a controller.

Float · practical issues

Flying capacitor in practice

Start-up: pre-charge

Cfc must reach Vdc/2 before PWM starts. With an empty capacitor the outer switch sees the full Vdc (state 11: S1′ blocks Vdc − Vfc). A pre-charge circuit or a controlled start-up sequence is needed.

Measurement and control

Active balancing needs Vfc measured in every leg, on a floating node. Phase-shifted carriers give “natural” balancing through the load impedance at fsw, but it is slow and load-dependent.

Size vs. switching frequency

Cfc ∝ I / (fsw ΔV): bulky at the low fsw of MV IGBTs, small at the high fsw of SiC and GaN. This is why flying-capacitor converters are popular in high-frequency DC–DC and GaN designs.

Faults

A discharged or shorted Cfc puts the full Vdc across devices rated for Vdc/2. Protection must detect Vfc leaving its band and stop switching.

Design example

Design walk-through: one leg for a 1500 V DC link

  1. Number of levels. Devices may block ≤ 2/3 of their rating. 2-level: 1500 V → 3.3 kV class (slow). 3-level: 750 V → 1200 V class.
  2. Which clamp. T-type outer switches still block 1500 V ✗. NPC works; ANPC if the converter must also run as a rectifier (loss sharing) → 3L ANPC.
  3. Or five levels. 5L-ANPC: selectors block 750 V (1200 V class), cell devices block 375 V (650 V class), Vfc = 375 V.
  4. Size the flying capacitor. Allow ΔVfc,pp = 5 % of 375 V ≈ 19 V at Ipk = 100 A, fsw = 10 kHz → Cfc ≥ 100 / (2 · 104 · 19) ≈ 270 µF.
  5. Check the balancing. Midpoint: authority vs. power factor (Part II). Vfc: redundant-state selection. Verify both in simulation before building.

Where the Cfc rule comes from

With phase-shifted carriers a redundant state lasts at most half a switching period and carries the full phase current. From Cfc dVfc/dt = ±ia:

ΔVfc,pp ≤ Ipk (Ts/2) / Cfc
Cfc ≥ Ipk / (2 fsw ΔVfc,pp)
Question: what happens to Cfc if you double fsw? Why do GaN flying-capacitor converters get away with small ceramic capacitors?

Basics · carrier-based PWM of one leg · read: Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, Ch. 8

Basics: sine-triangle PWM (SPWM)

Carrier c(t): a triangle between −1 and +1 at the switching frequency fc.

Reference (modulating signal) r(t) = ma sin ω1t: the wanted pole voltage, normalized to Vdc/2.

Comparator: S1 = 1 while r > c, so vaO = ±Vdc/2. The pulse width follows the reference.

Duty cycle in one carrier period (r ≈ constant), by similar triangles:

d = (1 + r)/2
⟨vaO⟩ = d · Vdc/2 + (1 − d)(−Vdc/2) = r · Vdc/2

The local average (blue) reproduces the reference. Everything else is harmonics near multiples of fc.

Question: 800 V link, r = 0.5 at this instant. What are the duty cycle and the average pole voltage? d = 0.75 and ⟨vaO⟩ = 0.5 · 400 V = +200 V.

2.5 min. Build it click by click: carrier → reference → comparator → duty cycle and local average. Then press P to run time: the zoom window follows the cursor and the pulse width breathes with the sine. Drag on the top plot to place the cursor by hand.

  • Push ma above 1: the reference leaves the carrier, pulses drop out and the local average clips at ±400 V (next slide).
  • Recap of the undergraduate leg; Part I used it on the two-level leg. The multilevel carrier lab generalizes the comparator: N − 1 carriers, count how many the reference exceeds.
Basics · ma, mf and the PWM spectrum · read: Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, Ch. 8

Basics: modulation index and frequency ratio

ma = V̂r / V̂c   amplitude modulation index
mf = fc / f1   frequency modulation ratio

Linear range, ma ≤ 1: the fundamental is exactly

V̂1 = ma · Vdc/2

Overmodulation, ma > 1: pulses drop out, V̂1 grows more slowly than ma, and low-order harmonics (3, 5, 7) appear. The limit is the square wave, V̂1 = (4/π) Vdc/2.

Harmonics sit in clusters at h = j mf ± k (j odd: k even; j even: k odd). Choose mf odd (no even harmonics) and a multiple of 3 (the carrier harmonic is common mode and cancels in vab).

Question: f1 = 50 Hz, mf = 15. Where is the first cluster, and what is left of it in vab? Around 750 Hz: h = 13, 15, 17. h = 15 is triplen and cancels in vab; 650 Hz and 850 Hz remain.

2.5 min. Sweep ma from 0.5 to 3: the red dot leaves the straight line at 1 and creeps up to 4/π. In the spectrum, nothing appears below the carrier cluster in the linear range; above 1, the 3rd, 5th and 7th appear.

  • mf 15 → 16: even harmonics appear (no half-wave symmetry). Then switch pole ↔ line-to-line: h = 15 disappears from vab.
  • Rule of thumb (Mohan): for small mf keep it an odd integer and a multiple of 3 (synchronous PWM); above about 21 asynchronous PWM is acceptable.
  • In three-phase, min-max injection extends the linear range to 2/√3 ≈ 1.155 (zero-sequence slide, three slides later).
Modulate

From a reference to gate signals

① Which level, when?

The modulator turns a continuous reference into a sequence of levels whose switching-period average equals the reference.

Example: 3L NPC, reference r = 0.6. Within one carrier period the leg sits in P for 60 % and in O for 40 % of the time → average 0.6 · Vdc/2 = 240 V on an 800 V link.

Carrier comparison and space vectors are two views of the same averaging.

② Which state for that level?

When several states give the same level, the choice is free for the output — so we spend it on something else: midpoint balance, Vfc, or losses.

From Part I: ANPC zero → OU or OL. FC zero → 10 (charges Cfc if ia > 0) or 01 (discharges). 5L-ANPC +200 V → “a” or “b”.

Rule: decide the level from the reference, then the state from the capacitor error and the current sign.

1.5 min. The two-stage picture is the key idea of Part II: separate the output decision (level) from the internal decision (state). For a 2-level leg the first stage is exactly the SPWM comparator of the last two slides.

Balance

Why capacitors drift

Split DC link (NPC, T-type, ANPC, 5L)

Vdc = vC1 + vC2    ΔV = vC1 − vC2
C dΔV/dt = iO    iO = Σx (1 − |rx|) ix

Ideal, balanced operation gives zero average iO but a ripple at 3f1. Device mismatch, leakage, transients and unbalanced loads integrate into drift.

Flying capacitor (FC, 5L-ANPC)

Cfc dVfc/dt = (S1 − S2) ia

Every redundant state moves charge. Alternating them keeps the average at zero but does not remove an existing error.

An off-target Vfc distorts the levels (P − Vfc ≠ 0) and overstresses the devices that block it.

Sum ≠ difference. The DC-voltage controller (or source) fixes vC1 + vC2. Only current through O — chosen by the modulator — moves ΔV. Same for Vfc: only the redundant-state choice moves it.

2 min. Both equations come straight from Part I’s switching functions. Balancing is a modulator job, not a DC-controller job.

Balance · analysis · read: Celanovic & Boroyevich, IEEE TPEL 2000

Why an offset can balance the midpoint — and when it cannot

Add a common offset r0 to all three references (line-to-line voltages unchanged):

iO = Σx (1 − |rx + r0|) ix

Sensitivity to a small offset:

∂iO/∂r0 = −Σx sign(rx) ix

Average over a period with rx = m cos θx, ix = I cos(θx − φ):

⟨sign(cos θ) cos(θ − φ)⟩ = (2/π) cos φ
⟨∂iO/∂r0⟩ = −(6/π) I cos φ

Close the loop with r0 = K · sign(P) · ΔV:

C dΔV/dt = −(6/π) I K |cos φ| ΔV
τ = πC / (6 I K |cos φ|)

C = 4.7 mF, I = 50 A, K = 0.0015 V−1 → τ ≈ 33 ms at unity PF; τ → ∞ at PF = 0.

  • The authority is proportional to active power: it is lost at low power factor.
  • Its sign follows the power direction: a fixed-sign gain is unstable in rectifier mode.
Question: check both claims in the simulation on the next slide.

3 min. Derive on the board; the simulation then confirms it (set φ = 90°, then φ = 180° with the naive sign).

Balancing the midpoint with an offset

5 min. 2 × 4.7 mF, 50 A peak. Starts at 420 / 380 V with the loop on. Speed ×0.1 so the 150 Hz ripple is visible.

  1. Loop off → ΔV stays offset (no natural restoring force). Add 1 A leakage → it drifts.
  2. Loop on → r0 moves, average iO goes negative, ΔV returns with τ ≈ 33 ms. PI removes the leakage offset.
  3. φ = 90° → authority ≈ 0 %: kick ΔV and watch it stay.
  4. φ = 180° with the naive sign → positive feedback → trip. With sign(P) it works. (Printed quiz, question 4.)
Balance · flying capacitor in operation

Flying capacitor: three ways to choose

Alternate 10/01: zero net charge per pair — but press Disturb: the error stays.
Always 10: Vfc follows the current — a swing at the fundamental frequency.
Balance: sign(ia)·(Vfc* − Vfc) picks the state — the error is gone within a few switching periods.

2.5 min. With an ideal current source, phase-shifted PWM has no restoring force; “natural balancing” in real converters comes from the load impedance at the switching frequency and is slow. Active selection is fast.

Control · modelling (same local averaging as the Control & Modelling module)

Averaged model: what the current controller sees

Average pole voltage of any of our legs, offset included:

⟨vxO⟩ = (rx + r0) Vdc/2,   x ∈ {a, b, c}

Balanced three-wire load: the floating neutral removes the common part, so r0 disappears:

⟨vxn⟩ = ⟨vxO⟩ − ⅓ Σy⟨vyO⟩ = rx Vdc/2

Filter L, R to the grid voltage e:

L di/dt = v − e − R i

In a frame rotating with the grid (amplitude-invariant Park, d aligned with e):

L did/dt = vd − ed − R id + ωL iq
L diq/dt = vq − eq − R iq − ωL id

Choose vd = ud + ed − ωL iq and vq = uq + eq + ωL id: each axis becomes L di/dt + R i = u, controlled by a PI with Kp = ωcL, Ki = ωcR.

To the controller, the multilevel leg is just a voltage source with the limit |v| ≤ Vdc/√3 (with min-max). The number of levels changes the ripple, not this model. Question: where does the ωL coupling come from? From differentiating in a rotating frame: the derivative of the rotation produces the cross terms.

Summary

Three things to remember

1 · More levels change the steps. Clamp, float or combine: each device sees Vdc/(N−1); the output gets smaller steps and smaller harmonics.

2 · Redundancy is a control input. Several states give the same voltage — the choice balances capacitors, shares losses, or both.

3 · It only works as a system. Topology, modulation, balancing and current control have to be designed together — and checked in both power directions.
