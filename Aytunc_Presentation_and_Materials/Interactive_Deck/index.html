Basics · carrier-based PWM of one leg · read: Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, Ch. 7–8

Basics: one comparator, from DC-DC to sine PWM

Carrier: a triangle at the switching frequency fc.

Reference: the voltage we want, as a fraction of the DC link. Start with a constant, a DC reference, as in a buck converter.

Comparator: the switch is on while the reference is above the carrier. The output jumps between the two rails; only the pulse width changes.

Average over one switching period (blue) equals the reference: a reference of 0.6 gives 60 % on-time, and 480 V on an 800 V link. This is the DC-DC converter you already know.

Now let the reference move: a sine, slow compared with the carrier, and a carrier from −1 to +1 around the DC-link midpoint. Same comparator: the pulse width follows the sine and the local average reproduces it. That is an inverter leg. Everything else is harmonics near multiples of fc.

2.5 min. Build it click by click: carrier → DC reference → comparator → average (buck). Click 4 switches the same simulation to a sine reference. Press P to run time: in DC mode the pulses are all the same width; in sine mode the pulse width breathes with the sine and the zoom window follows the cursor. Drag on the top plot to place the cursor by hand.

  • DC mode: move D and watch the average and the readout (on-time × 800 V). The output is measured from the negative rail, as in a buck converter.
  • Sine mode: the pole voltage is measured from the midpoint, so it swings ±400 V. Push ma above 1: the reference leaves the carrier, pulses drop out and the average clips at ±400 V (slides 5 and 6).
  • Message: the modulator does not care what the reference is. DC gives a DC-DC converter, a sine gives an inverter, and the controller later on simply hands it whatever voltage it needs.
  • Sefer used the same leg; the multilevel carrier lab generalizes the comparator to N − 1 carriers.
Basics · one switching period, step by step · read: Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, Ch. 8

One switching period: why the average is r · Vdc/2

① The leg makes only two voltages

Measured from the DC-link midpoint O: upper switch on gives +Vdc/2, lower switch on gives −Vdc/2. Nothing in between.

② r is the voltage we want

Written as a fraction of Vdc/2: r = +1 means +Vdc/2, r = 0 means 0 V, r = −1 means −Vdc/2. Rule from the last slide: upper switch on while r is above the carrier, lower switch on otherwise.

③ Inside one period, r is a constant

The carrier is much faster than the sine (e.g. 15 carrier periods per sine cycle), so during one carrier period the sine barely moves. Take r = 0.5 for this period.

④ Why the switch is on for (1 + r)/2 of the period

The carrier is a straight line between −1 and +1, so it spends equal time at every height. The switch is on while the carrier is below r: the stretch from −1 up to r, which is 1 + r out of a total height of 2.

d = (1 + r)/2  r = 0.5 → 75 % · r = 0 → 50 % · r = 1 → 100 %

⑤ The average is r · Vdc/2

The load (an inductor, a motor) is too slow to follow the pulses; it responds to their average over the period:

d · (+Vdc/2) + (1 − d) · (−Vdc/2) = (2d − 1) · Vdc/2 = r · Vdc/2

800 V link, r = 0.5: 0.75 × 400 V − 0.25 × 400 V = +200 V.

3 min. One question per click; ask it out loud before you click.

  • ① “What can the leg output?” Only two values. Everything else is built from time: how long we stay at each.
  • ② “What is r?” Just the wanted voltage, scaled so that ±1 is the most the leg can give. On the last slide (buck) the carrier went 0 … 1; here it goes −1 … +1 because the output must also go negative.
  • ③ “Why can we treat r as constant?” One carrier period is 1/15 of the sine cycle (mf = 15). The bigger mf, the better this is.
  • ④ “Why (1 + r)/2?” Point at the figure: the carrier falls from +1 to −1 and rises back, at constant speed. The fraction of time it is below r equals the fraction of its height below r: 1.5 of 2 → 75 %. Check the ends: r = 1 → always on; r = −1 → never on. Same as the buck on the last slide, where the carrier went 0 … 1 and the on-time was simply D.
  • ⑤ “Why the average?” An inductor current changes with the voltage’s area over time; if the pulses are fast, it only sees the net area per period, i.e. the average. Substitute ④: 2d − 1 = r.
  • Close: “Average output = r × Vdc/2, in every switching period.” Next slide lets r be a sine.
Basics · ma, mf and the PWM spectrum · read: Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, Ch. 8

From one period to the sine: ma, mf and the fundamental

⑥ Let r follow a sine

r = ma sin ωt. ma is the sine’s peak compared with the carrier’s peak (1); here ma = 0.8.

⑦ Why the fundamental is ma · Vdc/2

Every switching period gives its own average r · Vdc/2 (last slide). Put the periods side by side: the averages trace ma · Vdc/2 · sin ωt, the blue curve.

V̂1 = ma · Vdc/2  800 V, ma = 0.8 → 320 V peak

What is left (pulses minus their average) averages to zero in every period, so it only holds fast frequencies: the red groups in the spectrum, near mf, 2mf, … Nothing lands on the fundamental.

⑧ What happens when ma > 1

Sweep ma above 1: the red dot leaves the straight line, the curve bends towards 1.27, and harmonics 3, 5, 7 appear. Next slide: why.

⑨ mf: carrier periods per sine cycle

Bigger mf: the red groups move higher (easier to filter), and “r is constant within a period” is more accurate.

2.5 min. One question per click.

  • ⑥ “What is ma?” The size of the sine relative to the carrier. ma = 1: the sine just touches the carrier’s peaks.
  • ⑦ “Why is the fundamental ma·Vdc/2?” Last slide gave the average per period; a sine of averages is a sine. In the simulation the readout shows 320 V and the red dot sits on the straight line. “Why does nothing else land on f1?” The remainder has zero area in every switching period, so it changes much faster than the sine: its frequencies are around the carrier and its multiples (red bars).
  • ⑧ Only show it here: sweep ma to 3, the dot leaves the line, 3rd/5th/7th appear. The why is the next slide.
  • ⑨ mf: change it and watch the groups move. If asked: with mf a multiple of 3, the harmonic exactly at mf cancels in the line-to-line voltage (pole ↔ line-to-line button).
  • Three-phase min-max injection extends the linear range to 1.155: two slides later.
Basics · overmodulation in the average picture · read: Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, Ch. 8

Overmodulation: why the fundamental bends, and where it stops

① The on-time cannot exceed 100 %

When r > 1 the reference is above the whole carrier, so the switch stays on for the whole period. The average stays at +Vdc/2 = 400 V, not r · Vdc/2.

② So the average is a clipped sine

ma = 1.3 asks for 520 V peak. Below 400 V the average follows the sine; around the peak it is held flat at 400 V (blue).

③ How much fundamental is in a wave?

Multiply the wave by a sine and add up the area:

V̂1 = (1/π) ∫02π v(θ) · sin θ dθ

Check: a pure sine of peak V gives (1/π) ∫ V sin²θ dθ = V.

④ Why the clipped wave gives more than 400 V

Compare it with a 400 V sine (grey), point by point over the positive half:

  • flanks: ma · 400 sin θ > 400 sin θ, because ma > 1
  • flat top: 400 ≥ 400 sin θ, because sin θ ≤ 1

It is never below the grey sine (the negative half mirrors this), so in ③ its area is larger: V̂1 > 400 V. At 1.3: 453 V, less than the 520 V asked for.

⑤ The limit is a square wave

(1/π) [ ∫0π 400 sin θ dθ − ∫π2π 400 sin θ dθ ] = 1600/π ≈ 509 V

= (4/π) · Vdc/2 = 1.27 · Vdc/2: the most this leg can give, because it is already at ±400 V all the time. It is reached only as ma → ∞; ma = 1.27 gives just 450 V.

⑥ The price: harmonics 3, 5, 7

The clipped wave is not a sine. Its two halves are mirror images, so only odd multiples appear: 150, 250, 350 Hz, too close to 50 Hz to filter.

3 min. One question per click, asked out loud.

  • ① “What happens to (1 + r)/2 when r = 1.2?” It says 110 %: impossible. The switch is simply on all period.
  • ② “So what does the average look like?” The sim at ma = 1.3: red dashed is what we asked for, blue is what we get.
  • ③ “How do we measure the fundamental of a non-sine?” The Fourier coefficient. The check with a pure sine shows it gives back the peak.
  • ④ “Why is 453 V more than 400 V?” The blue curve is everywhere at or above the grey 400 V sine. Same weight sin θ, more area, bigger fundamental. Clipping took area from the 520 V sine, but what is left still beats a 400 V sine.
  • ⑤ “What is the most we can get?” Click: the sim shows the square wave, 509 V. Point at the table: the fundamental creeps towards 509 V, only as ma → ∞. Do not confuse the 1.27 here (fundamental / (Vdc/2)) with an ma value. With real pulses the last thin pulses near the zero crossings vanish at a large but finite ma.
  • ⑥ Point at the harmonic bars: 3rd, 5th, 7th. Odd only, because the wave satisfies v(θ + π) = −v(θ). Formula behind the table, if asked: V̂1/(Vdc/2) = (2/π)[ma·asin(1/ma) + √(1 − 1/ma²)].
  • Message: above ma = 1 you trade waveform quality for at most 27 % more voltage. Min-max injection (next slide) gets 15 % of it for free, without these harmonics.
Modulate · three phases, zero-sequence injection · read: imperix, SPWM vs SVPWM

15 % more voltage for free: min-max injection

① Three legs, three references

ra, rb, rc: three sines 120° apart. Each leg follows its own reference (slide 4), measured from the DC midpoint O.

② The load only sees differences

A three-wire load is connected between the legs, so it only sees vab = vaO − vbO, and so on. Adding the same number to all three references cancels in every difference.

③ That number is r0

r0 is a common offset (zero-sequence) added to all three: r′x = rx + r0. The load does not see it; only the legs’ limit of ±1 does. So we can use it to keep all three inside ±1.

④ Min-max: centre the three

Shift so the highest and lowest are equal and opposite:

r0 = −(max + min) / 2

m = 1.1, θ = 0: r = (1.10, −0.55, −0.55) clips. r0 = −0.275 → (0.825, −0.825, −0.825): inside ±1, same differences (table).

⑤ The gain: 1.155, or 15 %

Worst instant: two phases equal and opposite, ±0.866 m. Inside ±1 while m ≤ 1/0.866 = 2/√3 ≈ 1.155. Up to there, no clipping and no 3rd, 5th, 7th.

This offset is space-vector PWM. Sine PWM plus the min-max offset gives exactly the pulses of a space-vector modulator. Next two slides: why.

3 min. One question per click.

  • ① “What changes with three phases?” Nothing per leg: each leg still follows its own reference.
  • ② “What does the load see?” Only line-to-line voltages. Show the table: the last column r′a − r′b is the same with and without injection.
  • ③ “What is r0?” One number, added to all three references at each instant. It moves the DC-midpoint-to-load-neutral voltage, which a three-wire load cannot see. It is free, as long as no reference leaves ±1.
  • ④ Click switches injection on at m = 1.1, θ = 0: the numbers on the slide appear in the table. Press “Sweep θ”: the bold curves stay inside the band; the dashed r0 is a triangle-like wave at 3f1.
  • ⑤ “Why 1.155?” At θ = 30° two references are ±0.866 m and the third is 0: min-max cannot help there, so 0.866 m ≤ 1. Move θ to 30° and m to 1.155 to show it.
  • Worked example: 650 V DC link, 400 VLL,rms grid → phase peak 326.6 V → m = 326.6/325 = 1.005. Sine PWM clips (max 398 VLL); with min-max the limit is 460 VLL (readout, change Vdc).
  • Third-harmonic injection reaches the same limit: a third harmonic of amplitude m/6 also allows m = 2/√3; min-max is the flat-topped version of the same idea. Mention it here; Elif uses it on the CHB.
  • Click 5, transition: “What we just did by hand, adding r0, is what a space-vector modulator does automatically.” Careful with the wording: it is the min-max offset that gives exactly the SVPWM pulses. A pure third harmonic (m/6) reaches the same 1.155 limit, but its pulses are slightly different; it is a close cousin, not SVPWM itself.
Modulate · before space vectors: what a switching state is

Switching states: three legs, two positions each

① Each leg is P or N

Upper switch on: the leg output is at P, +Vdc/2. Lower switch on: N, −Vdc/2. Never both: that would short the DC link.

② A state names all three legs

Three letters, in the order a, b, c. PNN: leg a at P, legs b and c at N. Two choices per leg: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 states.

③ The load sees the differences

PNN on an 800 V link: vab = +800 V, vbc = 0, vca = −800 V (table).

④ Two states give nothing

PPP and NNN: all legs at the same rail, so every line voltage is zero. These are the zero states; the other six are active.

⑤ Each state is one arrow

Pack the three voltages into one arrow (the same way as the reference on the next slide). The 6 active states give 6 arrows of equal length, 60° apart: the corners of a hexagon. The zero states sit in the centre.

A three-level leg has a third position, O (the DC midpoint): 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 states.

2.5 min. Build the vocabulary before any space vectors. Click the P/N buttons live, or press “Next state”.

  • ① “What can one leg do?” Two positions only. The red box is the switch that is on.
  • ② “How many combinations?” 2³ = 8. The name is just the list of positions, a then b then c.
  • ③ The table: pole voltages from O, line voltages between the legs. Only the second row reaches the load.
  • ④ Set PPP, then NNN: the arrow disappears, all line voltages are zero. Two ways to make zero: this choice comes back on the next slide.
  • ⑤ Press “Next state” through PNN → PPN → NPN → NPP → NNP → PNP: the arrow jumps 60° each time. The packing is v = ⅔(vaO + a vbO + a² vcO), a = ej120°; for PNN it gives ⅔ · 800 V = 533 V at 0°.
Modulate · space-vector PWM, step by step · read: Holmes & Lipo; Zhou & Wang, IEEE TIE 2002

Space-vector PWM: build the reference from the nearest states

① The reference as one arrow

Pack the three references the same way (a = ej120°):

v* = ⅔ (ra + a rb + a² rc) = m ejθ

Length m; as θ = ωt grows it rotates, one turn per output cycle.

② The inverter has only 7 dots

The states of the last slide. v* is almost always between them.

③ So mix them in time

In one switching period, stay a while on each of the two corners next to v* and the rest of the time on a zero state. The average arrow is v* (shaded triangle, time shares on the right).

m = 0.8 at 30°, half way between PNN and PPN: 34.6 % PNN, 34.6 % PPN, 30.7 % zero.

④ The limit is the inner circle

No zero time left when v* touches the circle inside the hexagon: m = 1.155, the min-max limit of slide 7.

⑤ Same pulses as min-max

Split the zero time equally between NNN and PPP, and each leg’s pulses are exactly sine PWM plus the min-max offset. Next slide: why.

2.5 min. One question per click.

  • ① “What is v*?” The three references at one instant, packed into one arrow, the same packing as the states on the last slide. For balanced sines the result is exactly m ejθ. Press Rotate v*: it turns once per 20 ms. A phasor is this arrow frozen at t = 0; v* actually turns (a space vector). Its projection on the horizontal axis is ra.
  • ② “Can the inverter make v* directly?” No: only the 7 dots. Scale: the corners are at 4/3 in units of Vdc/2, i.e. 533 V.
  • ③ “Then how?” The same trick as one leg on slide 4: switch fast and let the average do the work. Here the average of arrows. Drag v* around: the triangle and the three shares change. At 30°, m = 0.8: the two corners get equal time.
  • ④ “Why 1.155?” Drag v* to the circle at 30°: the zero share becomes 0. The inner circle radius is (4/3)·cos 30° = 1.155.
  • ⑤ The equal split of zero time between NNN and PPP is a common shift of all three legs: the min-max r0. If asked: Zhou & Wang 2002 prove the equivalence. For more levels (3-level button) there are more dots and the same idea; PD carriers with a modified offset give the same sequence (McGrath, Holmes & Lipo 2003).
Modulate · one switching period, three legs, one carrier · read: Zhou & Wang, IEEE TIE 2002

Why min-max gives the same pulses as SVPWM

① One period, three legs, one carrier

Each leg is P while its reference is above the carrier (slide 4). Over one period the references hardly move: three flat lines.

② The legs switch one after another

So the period is a sequence of states: NNN → PNN → PPN → PPP → PPN → PNN → NNN. These are the two corners next to v* and the two zero states: the SVPWM ingredients.

③ The active times are already right

PNN lasts (ra − rb)/2, PPN (rb − rc)/2. They depend only on differences, so they give the right line voltages, the same times SVPWM computes.

④ The offset only moves zero time

Adding r0 lifts all three lines together: the active states keep their length, and time moves between NNN (ends) and PPP (middle).

⑤ Min-max splits the zero time equally

NNN = PPP exactly when the lines are centred, r0 = −(max + min)/2. That is SVPWM’s equal zero split: same states, same times, same order.

Without the offset one zero state runs out first, at m = 1. With the equal split both run out together, at 1.155.

3 min. m = 0.8, θ = 10° (inside the PNN–PPN sector). Point at the state band on top of the plot at each click.

  • ① “What does one period look like with three legs?” Three flat references, one triangle.
  • ② “Why that order?” The carrier falls from +1: the highest reference (a) crosses first, then b, then c. Leg a turns on, then b, then c: NNN → PNN → PPN → PPP. On the way up they turn off in reverse.
  • ③ The algebra: leg x is P for (1 + rx)/2 of the period. PNN is the time a is P but b is not: (1 + ra)/2 − (1 + rb)/2 = (ra − rb)/2. Here 53.1 %. PPN: (rb − rc)/2 = 12.0 %. These are exactly the SVPWM shares (the hexagon on slide 9 gives the same numbers).
  • ④ Point at the dashed lines: dark blue marks the NNN ends, light blue the PPP middle; the line under the plot compares them. Without offset: NNN = (1 − ra)/2 = 10.6 %, PPP = (1 + rc)/2 = 24.3 %: unequal (red). Move θ or m: the active times change, the split stays unequal.
  • ⑤ Click switches the offset to min-max: the dashed PPP lines move closer together (PPP shrinks), the NNN lines move inwards (NNN grows), and the line turns green: NNN 17.4 % = PPP 17.4 %. r0 = −0.137; the active times between the dashed lines are unchanged. Setting NNN = PPP, (1 − ra − r0)/2 = (1 + rc + r0)/2, gives r0 = −(ra + rc)/2: the min-max formula.
  • Limit: push m to 1.1 with offset none: a reference leaves ±1, one zero state is gone (red message), although the other still has time. With min-max both reach zero together only at 1.155.
Modulate · sine PWM for a three-level leg · read: Holmes & Lipo

Three levels: one carrier for each half

① Two levels: one carrier

Reference above the carrier → P, below → N. One yes/no answer picks one of two levels (slide 4).

② Three levels: split into two halves

The leg can also sit at O, the midpoint (0 V). Positive reference: switch between P and O. Negative: between O and N. Each half gets its own carrier: upper 0 … +1, lower −1 … 0.

③ Inside each half: the same rule

Above the upper carrier → P, otherwise O. Below the lower carrier → N, otherwise O. Each half is slide 4 again, so the average is still r · Vdc/2, with jumps of only Vdc/2.

④ Two ways to draw the lower carrier

A shifted copy of the upper one (PD), or its mirror image (POD; that is using the same carrier for both halves). For one leg the result is the same. Next slide: why PD is used.

2.5 min. Only leg a is shown. Click 3 switches the lower carrier from shifted (PD) to mirrored (POD).

  • ① “Why not one carrier?” One comparator answers yes or no: two levels. Three levels need a second answer.
  • ② “Which second answer?” The sign of the reference picks the half. The labels in the plot show which carrier decides P/O and which O/N.
  • ③ Point at the output: positive half only P/O pulses (+400/0), negative half only O/N (0/−400). Fundamental readout 360 V = 0.9 × 400, as on slide 5.
  • ④ Click: the lower carrier flips. Pulses in the negative half move a little; the average does not. If asked about “one carrier per phase”: compare |r| with a 0 … 1 carrier and use the sign to pick P or N. That is exactly the mirrored lower carrier, POD.
Modulate · why PD is the usual choice · read: Holmes & Lipo; McGrath & Holmes, IEEE TIE 2002

PD or POD? Look at two phases at the same instant

① All phases share the same carriers

One set of carriers for the whole converter. Each phase compares its own reference with it.

② One phase up, one phase down

At θ = 60°: ra = +0.78 uses the upper carrier, rb = −0.78 the lower one. The load sees the difference, vab = vaO − vbO.

③ POD: both legs switch together

The lower carrier is the mirror image, so leg a goes to P exactly when leg b goes to N. vab jumps 0 ↔ 800 V at once.

④ PD: one leg at a time

The lower carrier is a shifted copy, with the same timing. Legs a and b switch at different instants: vab steps 400 ↔ 800 V. Half the jump, less ripple.

⑤ Over a full cycle

Lower line-voltage THD with PD (readout). Same fundamental, smaller ripple, smaller filter: that is why PD is the standard choice.

3 min. Three carrier periods at θ = 60°. Clicks 0–2 show POD, click 3 switches to PD.

  • ① “Does each phase get its own carriers?” No: one set, shared. That is what makes the comparison between phases possible.
  • ② “Which carrier does each phase use?” At 60° a is positive and b negative, so they use different carriers. That is the case where PD and POD differ.
  • ③ Point at the two pole-voltage rows: the pulses line up, and vab goes 0 → 800 V in one step. Read the “biggest jump” readout: 800 V.
  • ④ Click: the lower carrier is now a copy. b’s pulses move by half a period relative to a’s, so vab only steps by 400 V; ripple readout drops.
  • ⑤ Full-cycle readout: PD vs POD line THD. Move θ to 100°: both a and b positive, same carrier, no difference. The difference comes only from phases in opposite halves, which happens all the time in three-phase.
  • Why “in phase” is the name: with PD all carriers move up and down together; for a single phase the pulses are the same as POD, for the line voltage they are not.
Modulate · more levels · read: Holmes & Lipo

More levels: more carriers, same rule

① Five levels: four carriers, same rule

Stack four carriers between −1 and +1. The level is how many carriers are below the reference: 0 → −400 V, … 4 → +400 V, in steps of 200 V. N levels need N − 1 carriers.

② PD still wins

Mirror the lower two carriers (POD) and the line THD rises from 18 % to 30 %: same reason as the last slide, phases in opposite halves switch together.

1.5 min. Five levels, all three phases, line voltage. Click 1 switches to POD.

  • ① Same counting rule as for three levels, just more carriers. Point at the finer staircase in vab.
  • ② Read the THD: PD 18.1 %, POD 29.8 %. If asked about APOD (every carrier mirrored against its neighbour): with three levels it is identical to POD; with five it gives 28.5 %, almost the same as POD.
  • Phase-shifted carriers are left to Elif’s CHB talk.
Modulate → Balance · the free choice that balances the capacitors

Same output, two ways: the free choice

① The DC link is two capacitors

In a three-level converter the DC link is split: C1 on top, C2 below, and the midpoint O between them. Each leg can connect its output to P, O or N.

② Two states, the same output

POO: leg a at P, legs b and c at O. ONN: leg a at O, legs b and c at N. The load sees exactly the same line voltages (table): +400 V, 0, −400 V.

③ Why they look the same

ONN is POO with every leg moved down by one level. Moving all legs together is the common offset r0 of slide 7: the load cannot see it.

④ But the midpoint can

A leg at O takes its current from the midpoint. POO: b and c are at O, 10 A flows into O. ONN: a is at O, 10 A flows out of O. One state charges C1 and discharges C2, the other does the opposite.

⑤ So the modulator decides twice

First which voltage (the carriers of the last slides). Then, where two states give it, which state: the one that pushes the capacitor voltages back to equal. In carrier terms that is a small r0: the balancing knob of the next slides.

3 min. The figure is one instant: ia = +10 A, ib = ic = −5 A. Click 3 switches from POO to ONN; you can also press the POO / ONN buttons or set each leg by hand.

  • ① “Where is O?” Point at the two capacitors: 400 V each on an 800 V link. O is the node between them; each leg is a three-position switch.
  • ② “Can two different states give the same output?” Read the table for POO, then press ONN: the second row does not change.
  • ③ “Why?” Every pole voltage drops by 400 V; differences stay. Same idea as r0 on slide 7, now in whole levels.
  • ④ “So what is different?” Point at the blue midpoint rail. POO: legs b and c hang on O and their −5 A + −5 A come back into O. ONN: leg a hangs on O and draws its +10 A out of O. Readout: which capacitor rises.
  • ⑤ The rule for the rest of the talk: decide the voltage from the reference, then the state from the capacitor error and the current direction. The same choice exists inside one flying-capacitor leg (states 10 / 01), which comes after the neutral-point slides.
Balance · the normal ripple

Why the capacitor voltages wiggle at 150 Hz

① The midpoint current goes up and down

Legs at O send their current through the midpoint (slide 14). Over one cycle this current rises and falls (plot).

② It repeats three times per cycle

The three phases are identical, just shifted by a third of a cycle. So what they make together repeats every third of a cycle: 3 × 50 Hz = 150 Hz.

③ The capacitors follow it

C1 and C2 wiggle at 150 Hz, a few volts, in opposite directions. It averages to zero, so it never grows. Bigger capacitors, smaller wiggle.

1.5 min. One output cycle, 20 ms; 50 A, m = 0.8, unity power factor.

  • ① Just show the shape. No formula needed.
  • ② “Why three?” Click: the dashed lines cut the cycle into thirds; the curve is the same in each. Phase b is phase a delayed by a third of a cycle, phase c by two thirds. Anything built equally from all three cannot tell the difference after a third of a cycle, so it repeats. (The same reason the min-max offset on slide 7 repeats at 150 Hz.)
  • ③ Click: the capacitor voltages. Readout about 4.6 V peak-to-peak with 4.7 mF each. Drag C down: the wiggle grows. This is normal and handled by choosing C; the balancing loop does not try to remove it.
  • Formula if asked: a leg is at O for (1 − |r|) of each period, so iO = Σ (1 − |rx|)·ix.
Balance · why the midpoint needs a controller

Why the two capacitors drift apart

① Ideal case: only the wiggle

Last slide: with a balanced load the midpoint current has no average. The two voltages wiggle at 150 Hz around 400 V and stay there.

② But nothing pulls them back

A small error (a leaky capacitor, a sensor offset, a load step) adds up, and the voltages drift apart until the converter trips. The DC source only holds the total, 800 V, not how it is split.

③ So the modulator must steer it

The only handle is the midpoint current, and slide 14 showed how to choose it: POO or ONN, that is the offset r0. Next: how it moves the charge.

2 min. No balancing in this simulation; we only watch the two capacitor voltages (2 × 4.7 mF, 50 A peak, unity power factor, speed ×0.1).

  • ① Point at the two lines: the 150 Hz wiggle of the last slide, no trend.
  • ② Click: 1 A of leakage on the top capacitor. C1 sinks, C2 rises, until the trip line. With leakage 0, press “Kick”: the voltages just stay where they were pushed. No restoring force, like a ball on a flat table.
  • ③ The DC controller only sees vC1 + vC2. The difference needs its own knob: the midpoint current, chosen by the modulator.
  • If asked for the formula: a leg sits at O for (1 − |r|) of each period, so ⟨iO⟩ = Σ (1 − |rx|)·ix, and C·d(vC1 − vC2)/dt = ⟨iO⟩.
Balance · what the offset does inside one switching period

How the offset pushes charge into or out of the midpoint

① One instant, no offset

Leg c is positive (+43 A), leg b negative (−43 A). Both sit at O for 31 % of the period, so their currents through O cancel: no midpoint current.

② Lift all references: r0 = +0.1

Every leg moves up. Leg c: more time at P, less at O. Leg b: less time at N, more at O. Now b’s negative current wins: current flows into the midpoint. C1 goes down, C2 goes up.

③ Lower them: r0 = −0.1

The opposite: c spends more time at O, b less. Current flows out of the midpoint. C1 goes up, C2 goes down.

④ The load sees nothing

All legs moved together, so the line voltages do not change (slide 7). So: C1 too high → r0 > 0; C2 too high → r0 < 0. That is exactly what the PI controller on the next slide does.

2.5 min. One instant, when leg a crosses zero (r = 0, no current, so it does not matter where it sits). Each bar is one switching period: red P, grey O, blue N. The dashed box marks the time at O without offset. Clicks set r0 = 0, +0.1, −0.1; the slider and buttons do the same.

  • ① Time at O is 1 − |r| (slide 11): 1 − 0.69 = 31 % for both b and c. +43 A × 31 % and −43 A × 31 % cancel.
  • ② c: r = 0.79, O 21 %, +9 A through O. b: r = −0.59, O 41 %, −18 A. Sum −9 A: into O.
  • ③ The mirror image: +9 A, out of O.
  • ④ This works because, while delivering power, the positive leg carries the positive current. With reactive current or as a rectifier the push changes or reverses; the controller then uses r0 = sign(P)·PI(ΔV).
Balance · closing the loop with a PI controller

Balancing the midpoint with a PI controller

① Measure the difference

ΔV = vC1 − vC2. The goal is zero.

② A PI controller makes the offset

ΔV goes into a PI controller; its output is the offset r0 added to all three references (last slide). The P part reacts at once, the I part keeps pushing until ΔV is exactly zero.

③ Loop on: the voltages come back

C1 and C2 return to 400 V within a few cycles. The 150 Hz wiggle stays: it is normal (slide 15).

④ A leak? The I part handles it

With a 1 A leak the I part settles at the small offset that cancels it, and ΔV stays at zero.

3 min. Starts at 420 / 380 V with the loop off: the voltages just stay apart (slide 16). Click 2 switches the PI loop on, click 3 adds 1 A of leakage. Speed ×0.1.

  • ② Gains: Kp = 0.0015 per volt, Ki = 0.03 per volt-second; the offset is limited to ±0.2 so it never pushes a reference out of the carrier band.
  • ③ Point at the lower plot: r0 jumps, then decays as ΔV closes. Time constant about 33 ms here.
  • ④ Without the I part a leak would leave a small permanent ΔV; with it, r0 settles at a small constant value.
  • If asked: the offset only has grip when the converter delivers active power (strength ∝ I·cos φ). With pure reactive current it cannot balance, and as a rectifier the push reverses, so the controller multiplies by sign(P): r0 = sign(P)·PI(ΔV). The full lab (button “Loop” and φ) is in the source if you want to show it.
Balance · the same idea inside one leg

Flying capacitor: two ways to make 0 V

① Two ways to make 0 V

A capacitor sits between the switch pairs, charged to 400 V. State 10 and state 01 both put the output at 0 V.

② They push it opposite ways

Follow the current: in 10 it charges the capacitor, in 01 it discharges it. Same output, opposite effect.

③ Just taking turns

After a disturbance the capacitor is 60 V low. Taking 10 and 01 in turn does not fix it: the error stays.

④ Choose by the error

Too low? Pick the state that charges it. Too high? The one that discharges it. Back at 400 V in a few periods.

2 min. Same idea as POO / ONN on the NPC, but inside one leg.

  • ① State 10 = S1 and S2′ on; state 01 = S2 and S1′ on. In both, the output sits at +400 − 400 = 0 V (through the capacitor).
  • ② Click shows state 01: the current now flows through the capacitor the other way. For ia > 0, 10 charges and 01 discharges; when the current reverses, the rule flips.
  • ③ Alternating keeps the average charge zero, so an error that is already there is never removed.
  • ④ The rule: pick 10 if sign(ia)·(Vfc* − Vfc) > 0, else 01. In practice ΔV per period = i·t0/Cfc; the plot uses 10 V per period for clarity.
Grid · step 1 of 5

Grid connection: what makes the current flow?

① Equal voltages: no current

The converter copies the grid voltage. Nothing across the inductor, nothing flows.

② Leading: active power

3° ahead: the current is in step with the grid voltage. The leading side sends active power to the lagging side.

③ Higher voltage: reactive power

5 % bigger: the current comes a quarter period late. The higher-voltage side sends reactive power.

AC power flow: the angle sets the active power, the size sets the reactive power.

2.5 min. Per phase: grid 327 V peak (400 V line-to-line), L = 2 mH (0.63 Ω at 50 Hz). The converter voltage here is the average over a switching period (slide 4): the pulses are forgotten.

  • ① Point at the middle plot: the difference is zero.
  • ② 3° is a difference of about 17 V; it gives 27 A and 13 kW. Move the timing slider the other way: power comes back from the grid.
  • ③ The inductor delays the current a quarter period behind the voltage across it. Formulas if asked: P = 3·E·V·sin δ / (2X), Q ≈ 3·E·(V − E) / (2X), with peak values.
  • The rule holds for any two AC sources joined by an inductance (a line, a transformer): active power goes from the leading side to the lagging side, reactive power from the higher voltage to the lower. Play with both sliders to show it, in both directions.
  • Lead into the next slide: a few volts already move tens of amps, so the voltage cannot be set by hand.
Grid · step 2 of 5

Let a PI controller set the voltage

① By hand: it does not hold

We pick the voltage for 30 A. Then the grid drops 5 %, and the current is far off.

② Measure and correct

The PI compares the current with the wanted one and corrects the voltage. The drop is hardly seen.

③ But always a little late

A sine is a moving target. A small error stays (bottom plot). The dq transformation fixes this.

2.5 min. One phase shown. Wanted current: 30 A from 10 ms. Grid drop of 5 % at 50 ms.

  • ① 5 % of 327 V is 16 V across 0.63 Ω: about 26 A of extra current, plus a slowly decaying offset.
  • ② Converter voltage = measured grid voltage + PI correction. Gains: Kp = ωcL, Ki = ωcR, bandwidth 400 Hz.
  • ③ The error is about 12 % (50 Hz / 400 Hz): the PI needs an error to keep moving its output. Next slide removes it.
Grid · step 3 of 5

The dq transformation: sines become constants

① Three sines, one turning arrow

Like the voltage arrow of slide 9: the three currents are one arrow, turning at 50 Hz.

② The grid voltage is the reference

The dq frame turns with the grid voltage, which lies on the d axis. In this frame the currents are two constants.

③ id: active, iq: reactive

id is in phase with the grid voltage: active power. iq is 90° away: reactive power.

④ One PI for each

Constant targets: the error goes to zero. The two PI outputs are transformed back to three phases for the PWM.

2.5 min. Press P to pause the arrows.

  • ① The red sine in the bottom plot is phase a; the grey ones are b and c.
  • ② How it works: a PLL locks onto the measured grid voltage and gives its angle θ. With θ we transform the grid voltage and the currents (abc → dq, the Park transform). The grid voltage becomes ed = 327 V, eq = 0: that is what “reference” means.
  • ③ P = 3/2 · ed · id and Q = −3/2 · ed · iq (the sign of Q depends on the convention). Move the sliders: id changes P only, iq changes Q only. In the top-left picture, id makes the arrow longer along the grid voltage, iq turns it away from it.
  • ④ The whole chain: measure → abc to dq → PI on id and PI on iq → vd, vq → dq to abc → references for the carriers. The d and q axes are coupled through ωL; the controller cancels that term. The wanted id comes from the DC-voltage PI (next slide); the wanted iq is the reactive power we want, often zero.
Grid · step 4 of 5

Who decides the wanted current? The DC voltage

① The DC capacitor is a bucket

Power in fills it, power out empties it. In = out: the voltage stays.

② More comes in

Same current to the grid: the voltage climbs until the converter trips.

③ A PI watches the voltage

Too high? It asks for more id: more active power to the grid. The voltage comes back to 800 V.

2 min. 2 mF at 800 V. Power in goes from 10 kW to 20 kW at 20 ms.

  • ② 10 kW too much raises the voltage by about 6 V every millisecond: 900 V is reached 17 ms later.
  • ③ The output of this PI is the wanted id of the previous slide. It is much slower than the current loop (tens of Hz against several hundred Hz), so the two do not disturb each other.
  • This loop holds the sum of the two capacitor voltages; the midpoint PI of slide 18 holds their difference.

All together: three PI controllers

1 min. A recap; one block per click.

  • Speeds: current loop several hundred Hz; DC-voltage and midpoint loops 10–50 Hz.

Grid · step 5 of 5

Grid filter: cleaning up the current

① The pulses leave a ripple

The real converter voltage is pulses. A small zig-zag stays on the current.

② Three levels: half the ripple

Half the voltage jump, half the zig-zag. Same inductor.

③ LCL: almost none

A capacitor in the middle takes the ripple.

④ But an LCL can ring

After a kick it rings like a bell. A small resistor calms it.

2.5 min. 400 V, 50 kVA (102 A peak), 800 V DC, 10 kHz. L = 1.3 mH; LCL: 1 mH + 0.3 mH with 10 µF; R = 1.5 Ω.

  • Numbers: ripple about 7 A peak to peak (2-level, L) → 3.4 A (3-level, L) → below 1 A (LCL).
  • The ringing frequency is the LCL resonance, about 3.3 kHz here. Undamped, it can be excited by the switching or by the controller. The resistor costs only a few watts. It can also be damped in the controller (active damping).
  • Design rules if asked (Liserre, Blaabjerg & Hansen, IEEE TIA 2005): resonance between 10× grid frequency and half the switching frequency; capacitor reactive power below 5 % of the rating.
Industry and research

Where multilevel converters are used

Solar and batteries

1500 V PV and storage inverters use 3-level legs, so 1200 V devices can be used.

Motor drives and wind

Medium-voltage drives and wind converters: higher voltage with standard switches, cleaner output.

Research

Faster switches (SiC, GaN) in multilevel legs, and smarter control of the capacitor balance.

Summary

Three things to remember

1 · Pulses make an average. Compare the reference with a carrier; the average follows the reference. Min-max adds 15 % and is the same as SVPWM.

2 · More levels, more carriers. One carrier per step, all in phase (PD): smaller jumps, smaller ripple.

3 · The midpoint needs a controller. Some outputs can be made two ways; a PI controller picks the one that keeps C1 and C2 equal.
