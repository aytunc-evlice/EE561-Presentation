Learning objectives

After this talk you should be able to:

  1. Define carrier, reference, ma and mf, and predict the fundamental and the harmonic clusters of sine-triangle PWM.
  2. Generate the level sequence of a multilevel leg with carriers (PD, POD, APOD, phase-shifted).
  3. Compute the min-max offset and the linear limit, and explain why it is the space-vector modulator in carrier form.
  4. Explain why the split DC link drifts, and design an offset-based balancing loop, including its limits.
  5. Choose flying-capacitor states and MMC submodules in closed loop, and explain why an MMC needs circulating-current control.
  6. Place modulation and balancing inside a grid current controller, and compare L and LCL filters for 2- and 3-level legs.

Basics · carrier-based PWM of one leg · read: Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, Ch. 7–8

Basics: one comparator, from DC-DC to sine PWM

Carrier: a triangle at the switching frequency fc.

Reference: the voltage we want, as a fraction of the DC link. Start with a constant, a DC reference, as in a buck converter.

Comparator: the switch is on while the reference is above the carrier. The output jumps between the two rails; only the pulse width changes.

Average over one switching period (blue) equals the reference: a reference of 0.6 gives 60 % on-time, and 480 V on an 800 V link. This is the DC-DC converter you already know.

Now let the reference move: a sine, slow compared with the carrier, and a carrier from −1 to +1 around the DC-link midpoint. Same comparator: the pulse width follows the sine and the local average reproduces it. That is an inverter leg. Everything else is harmonics near multiples of fc.

2.5 min. Build it click by click: carrier → DC reference → comparator → average (buck). Click 4 switches the same simulation to a sine reference. Press P to run time: in DC mode the pulses are all the same width; in sine mode the pulse width breathes with the sine and the zoom window follows the cursor. Drag on the top plot to place the cursor by hand.

  • DC mode: move D and watch the average and the readout (on-time × 800 V). The output is measured from the negative rail, as in a buck converter.
  • Sine mode: the pole voltage is measured from the midpoint, so it swings ±400 V. Push ma above 1: the reference leaves the carrier, pulses drop out and the average clips at ±400 V (next slide).
  • Message: the modulator does not care what the reference is. DC gives a DC-DC converter, a sine gives an inverter, and the controller later on simply hands it whatever voltage it needs.
  • Sefer used the same leg; the multilevel carrier lab generalizes the comparator to N − 1 carriers.
Basics · ma, mf and the PWM spectrum · read: Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, Ch. 8

Basics: modulation index and frequency ratio

ma: sine peak / carrier peak.   mf: carrier frequency / output frequency.

Why the fundamental is ma · Vdc/2

The average over each switching period follows the reference (last slide), scaled to ±Vdc/2. So the average is a sine of peak ma · Vdc/2. The switching only adds harmonics far above it.

Overmodulation, ma > 1

Near the peak the sine is above the carrier: no pulses, the output sticks at +Vdc/2. The tops are clipped, so the fundamental grows more slowly than ma (at most 1.27 · Vdc/2, a square wave) and harmonics 3, 5, 7 appear.

Where the harmonics are

In groups around mf, 2mf, … A higher mf moves them further away: easier to filter. With mf a multiple of 3, the harmonic at mf cancels in the line voltage.

2.5 min. One idea per click.

  • Fundamental: point at the blue average on the last slide. The average over one switching period is the reference times Vdc/2; for a sine reference that average is a sine of peak ma·Vdc/2. The pulses add only high-frequency content around mf and its multiples, so nothing else lands on the fundamental. Show it in the simulation: at ma = 0.8 the red dot sits on the straight line (0.8 × 400 V = 320 V).
  • Overmodulation: sweep ma from 0.8 to 3. Above 1 the pulses near the peak disappear, the dot leaves the line and creeps up to 4/π ≈ 1.27, and the 3rd, 5th and 7th appear in the spectrum. Why: a flat-topped sine is a sine plus odd low-order harmonics, and clipping removes area from the peaks, so the fundamental is less than ma would predict.
  • Harmonics: back to ma = 0.8: nothing below the first group. Change mf and the groups move with it. Switch pole ↔ line-to-line: the harmonic exactly at h = mf = 15 disappears, because it is the same in all three phases; its neighbours (13, 17) stay. Choosing mf odd also avoids even harmonics (Mohan's rule for small mf; above about 21 it matters little).
  • In three-phase, min-max injection extends the linear range to 2/√3 ≈ 1.155 (min-max slide, three slides later).
Modulate

From a reference to gate signals

① Which level, when?

The modulator turns a continuous reference into a sequence of levels whose switching-period average equals the reference.

Example: 3L NPC, reference r = 0.6. Within one carrier period the leg sits in P for 60 % and in O for 40 % of the time → average 0.6 · Vdc/2 = 240 V on an 800 V link.

Carrier comparison and space vectors are two views of the same averaging.

② Which state for that level?

When several states give the same level, the choice is free for the output — so we spend it on something else: midpoint balance, Vfc, or losses.

From Sefer’s talk: ANPC zero → OU or OL. FC zero → 10 (charges Cfc if ia > 0) or 01 (discharges). 5L-ANPC +200 V → “a” or “b”.

Rule: decide the level from the reference, then the state from the capacitor error and the current sign.

1.5 min. The two-stage picture is the key idea of this talk: separate the output decision (level) from the internal decision (state). For a 2-level leg the first stage is exactly the SPWM comparator of the last two slides.

Carrier lab: PD, POD, APOD, phase-shifted

4.5 min. Chain: compare reference with N−1 carriers → count how many it exceeds → that is the level k. Same m and fc/f1 as on the basics slides.

  • Question: 5-level, PD vs POD — which gives lower line-to-line THD? PD: its carrier-frequency harmonics are in phase in all three legs, so they cancel in vab. Show vaO vs vab.
  • For 3 levels POD and APOD are identical (only two carriers).
  • This is the module’s main treatment of carrier PWM: define PD/POD/APOD and phase-shifted here in full. Elif’s CHB talk builds on it (cell-by-cell phase shift, band rotation, PS vs LS on the CHB), so she can refer back to this slide.

Modulate · zero-sequence injection · read: imperix, SPWM vs SVPWM

15 % more voltage for free: min-max injection

3 min. r0 = −[max(ra,rb,rc) + min(…)]/2 is added to all three phases. A three-wire load cannot see a common-mode voltage: line-to-line voltages are unchanged (table column).

Third-harmonic injection reaches the same limit: a third harmonic of amplitude m/6 also allows m = 2/√3; min-max is the flat-topped version of the same idea. Mention it here; Elif uses it on the CHB.

Worked example (on screen): 650 V DC link, 400 VLL,rms grid → phase peak 326.6 V → m = 326.6/325 = 1.005. Sine PWM clips (max 398 VLL); with min-max the limit is 460 VLL.

Modulate · the space-vector view in one slide · read: Zhou & Wang, IEEE TIE 2002; McGrath, Holmes & Lipo, IEEE TPEL 2003

Min-max on Sefer’s hexagon: the same modulator

Sefer’s map: 27 states → 19 vectors. A space-vector modulator builds v* from the three nearest vectors (shaded triangle) and their time shares.

Same limit: the dashed circle inscribed in the hexagon is |v*| = 2/√3 ≈ 1.155, the min-max limit of the last slide.
Same pulses: two-level centred SVPWM is sine-triangle PWM plus the min-max offset. For more levels, PD carriers plus a slightly modified offset give the nearest-three-vector sequence.
Redundancy is the offset: POO and ONN differ only by moving all three legs one level, a common-mode shift. Moving time between them is changing r0. So we balance with the offset, in the carrier world.

2 min. No dwell-time algebra needed in this talk. Drag the tip of v* on the hexagon: the shaded triangle shows the three nearest vectors, the bar their time shares, and the table the midpoint current of each state (POO: −ia, ONN: +ia).

  • Push m to 1.1: still inside the dashed circle, so still linear, exactly like min-max.
  • If asked how SVPWM computes the times: volt-second balance, d1V1 + d2V2 + d3V3 = v*, d1 + d2 + d3 = 1. The panel shows the result. Two-level equivalence: Zhou & Wang 2002. Multilevel: McGrath, Holmes & Lipo 2003 (PD carriers with a modified common-mode offset).
  • Why carriers here: one comparator per leg, and the offset r0 is directly the balancing knob of the next slides.
Balance · recap of Sefer’s ⟨iO⟩ = (1 − |r|) ia

Why capacitors drift

Split DC link (NPC, T-type, ANPC, 5L)

Vdc = vC1 + vC2    ΔV = vC1 − vC2
C dΔV/dt = iO    iO = Σx (1 − |rx|) ix

Ideal, balanced operation gives zero average iO but a ripple at 3f1. Device mismatch, leakage, transients and unbalanced loads integrate into drift.

Flying capacitor (FC, 5L-ANPC)

Cfc dVfc/dt = (S1 − S2) ia

Every redundant state moves charge. Alternating them keeps the average at zero but does not remove an existing error.

An off-target Vfc distorts the levels (P − Vfc ≠ 0) and overstresses the devices that block it.

Sum ≠ difference. The DC-voltage controller (or source) fixes vC1 + vC2. Only current through O — chosen by the modulator — moves ΔV. Same for Vfc: only the redundant-state choice moves it.

1 min. Do not re-derive: Sefer left ⟨iO⟩ = (1 − |r|)·ia and ifc = (S1 − S2)·ia on the board; summing over the three phases gives the left box. The message is the bottom line: balancing is a modulator job, not a DC-controller job.

Balance · analysis · read: Celanovic & Boroyevich, IEEE TPEL 2000

Why an offset can balance the midpoint — and when it cannot

Add a common offset r0 to all three references (line-to-line voltages unchanged):

iO = Σx (1 − |rx + r0|) ix

Sensitivity to a small offset:

∂iO/∂r0 = −Σx sign(rx) ix

Average over a period with rx = m cos θx, ix = I cos(θx − φ):

⟨sign(cos θ) cos(θ − φ)⟩ = (2/π) cos φ
⟨∂iO/∂r0⟩ = −(6/π) I cos φ

Close the loop with r0 = K · sign(P) · ΔV:

C dΔV/dt = −(6/π) I K |cos φ| ΔV
τ = πC / (6 I K |cos φ|)

C = 4.7 mF, I = 50 A, K = 0.0015 V−1 → τ ≈ 33 ms at unity PF; τ → ∞ at PF = 0.

  • The authority is proportional to active power: it is lost at low power factor.
  • Its sign follows the power direction: a fixed-sign gain is unstable in rectifier mode.
Question: check both claims in the simulation on the next slide.

3 min. Derive on the board; the simulation then confirms it (set φ = 90°, then φ = 180° with the naive sign).

Balance · simulation · 3-phase NPC, switching-period average model

Balancing the midpoint with an offset

3.5 min. 2 × 4.7 mF, 50 A peak. Starts at 420 / 380 V with the loop on. Speed ×0.1 so the 150 Hz ripple is visible.

  1. Loop off → ΔV stays offset (no natural restoring force). Add 1 A leakage → it drifts.
  2. Loop on → r0 moves, average iO goes negative, ΔV returns with τ ≈ 33 ms. PI removes the leakage offset.
  3. φ = 90° → authority ≈ 0 %: kick ΔV and watch it stay.
  4. φ = 180° with the naive sign → positive feedback → trip. With sign(P) it works. (Printed quiz, question 2c.)
Balance · flying capacitor in operation

Flying capacitor: three ways to choose

Alternate 10/01: zero net charge per pair — but press Disturb: the error stays.
Always 10: Vfc follows the current — a swing at the fundamental frequency.
Balance: sign(ia)·(Vfc* − Vfc) picks the state — the error is gone within a few switching periods.

2 min. Sefer showed that equal duties give zero net charge; this slide shows why that is not enough. With an ideal current source, phase-shifted PWM has no restoring force; “natural balancing” in real converters comes from the load impedance at the switching frequency and is slow. Active selection is fast.

MMC · modular multilevel converter · read: Lesnicar & Marquardt 2003; Debnath et al., IEEE TPEL 2015

MMC: one cell type, stacked into arms

Each arm: N half-bridge submodules (SMs) and an arm inductor. Each SM capacitor holds VC ≈ Vdc/N.

vu = Σk SkvCk ≈ nuVC,   vl ≈ nlVC
va = (vl − vu)/2 − (Larm/2) dia/dt

Keep nu + nl = N, so vu + vl ≈ Vdc. Nearest level: nu = round(N(1 − r)/2) gives N + 1 levels (or phase-shifted carriers, one per SM).

iu = ia/2 + ic,   il = −ia/2 + ic,   ic = Idc/3 + icirc

Every arm carries half the AC current plus its DC share: its capacitors charge and discharge every cycle. The balancing problem moves inside the arm.

Compared with the legs so far: no clamp diodes, no flying capacitors, no isolated sources (the CHB of the next talk needs them). One cell type, hundreds per arm in HVDC. The price: N capacitors per arm to keep equal, and an internal current ic to control.

2.5 min. Sign convention: iu flows from +Vdc/2 down through the upper arm, il from node a down to −Vdc/2; a positive arm current charges the inserted capacitors. Then ia = iu − il.

  • KVL: va = Vdc/2 − vu − L diu/dt = −Vdc/2 + vl + L dil/dt. Adding both gives the output equation on the slide; subtracting gives the circulating-current equation (slide after next).
  • Same two-stage idea as before: the modulator decides how many SMs (the level), the balancing decides which ones (the state).
MMC · simulation · one upper arm, nearest-level modulation · read: Tu, Xu & Xu, IEEE TPWRD 2011

MMC: which n of the N submodules? Sort them.

3 min. 6 kV DC link, N = 6 SMs of 1 kV, 3 mF each, 200 A peak AC, unity PF. The modulator gives n (how many); the rule picks which: if the arm current charges (iu > 0), insert the n lowest; if it discharges, the n highest.

  1. Starts with sort every period: all six SMs move together; only the common ripple at f1 and 2f1 remains. Point at the switching readout: about 2 kHz per SM, far more often than the level changes.
  2. Set speed to ×0.03, then fixed order: SM 1 is inserted almost all the time and collects the DC share of iu, so it charges; SM 6 is only in near the arm-voltage peak, where the arm current discharges it. They separate within about one cycle and trip; the frozen trace shows how. Same physics as the NPC midpoint: no natural restoring force. Reset.
  3. On level change, ±5 % band (Tu, Xu & Xu): only the SM entering or leaving is chosen by voltage, plus a full sort when the spread leaves the band. The spread now stays within a few percent, and each SM switches at about 100–200 Hz instead of 2 kHz. That is the trade-off: capacitor ripple against switching loss.
  4. φ = 180° (rectifier): sorting still works, because it uses the sign of the arm current, not the power direction. Compare with sign(P) in the NPC loop.
  5. Disturb SM 1 by −10 %, or age SM 2 (C −20 %): sorting absorbs both.
MMC · the internal current · read: Tu, Xu & Xu, IEEE TPWRD 2011; Debnath et al., IEEE TPEL 2015

MMC: the circulating current, and why it is controlled

Add the two arm equations of one phase:

Larm dic/dt = Vdc/2 − (vu + vl)/2

vu + vl is the sum of the inserted SM voltages. Their ripple (last slide) puts a 2f1 term in it, so ic gets a 2f1, negative-sequence component.

  • It flows between the phases: invisible at the AC output and in the DC current.
  • It raises the arm rms current: more conduction loss, more capacitor ripple, larger devices.

Give ic its own voltage vc*, common to both arms:

vu* = Vdc/2 − va* − vc*,   vl* = Vdc/2 + va* − vc*
→ Larm dic/dt = vc*,   va unchanged

An inductor driven by a voltage we choose: the same plant as the grid current loop.

  • Suppression: PI in a −2ω frame, or a resonant controller at 2f1, drives the 2f1 part to zero.
  • Energy: the DC part of ic sets the total arm energy (the slow loop in the simulation); its f1 part moves energy between upper and lower arm.
The same pattern as the NPC: the sum (DC voltage or total energy) and the difference (balance between capacitors) are separate loops, and the balancing uses an internal quantity the load never sees: the midpoint current there, the circulating current here.

2.5 min. Derivation: subtract the two KVL equations of the previous slide’s notes, with iu + il = 2ic. Adding a common vc* to both arm references cancels in va = (vl − vu)/2.

Why negative sequence at 2f1: the 2f1 ripple of phases a, b, c is shifted by 2 · 120°, which is the abc → acb order. In a dq frame rotating at −2ω it becomes DC, so a PI removes it (the CCSC of Tu, Xu & Xu).

Control · modelling (same local averaging as the Control & Modelling module)

Averaged model: what the current controller sees

Average pole voltage of any of our legs, offset included:

⟨vxO⟩ = (rx + r0) Vdc/2,   x ∈ {a, b, c}

Balanced three-wire load: the floating neutral removes the common part, so r0 disappears:

⟨vxn⟩ = ⟨vxO⟩ − ⅓ Σy⟨vyO⟩ = rx Vdc/2

Filter L, R to the grid voltage e:

L di/dt = v − e − R i

In a frame rotating with the grid (amplitude-invariant Park, d aligned with e):

L did/dt = vd − ed − R id + ωL iq
L diq/dt = vq − eq − R iq − ωL id

Choose vd = ud + ed − ωL iq and vq = uq + eq + ωL id: each axis becomes L di/dt + R i = u, controlled by a PI with Kp = ωcL, Ki = ωcR.

To the controller, the multilevel leg is just a voltage source with the limit |v| ≤ Vdc/√3 (with min-max). The number of levels changes the ripple, not this model. Question: where does the ωL coupling come from? From differentiating in a rotating frame: the derivative of the rotation produces the cross terms.

Current control in dq: what each term does

  • L did/dt = vd − ed − R id + ωL iq; L diq/dt = vq − eq − R iq − ωL id.

Control · the grid filter · read: Liserre, Blaabjerg & Hansen, IEEE TIA 2005; Peña-Alzola et al., IEEE TPEL 2013

Grid filter: L or LCL, and what the extra level buys

3 min. 400 V, 50 kVA, 800 V DC link, 10 kHz. Top: admittance from converter voltage to grid current. Bottom: grid-current harmonics = converter voltage harmonics × admittance, against 0.3 % of rated as a reference line.

  1. 2-level, L = 1.3 mH (start): the cluster at fsw reaches about 1.1 % of rated, ripple ≈ 1.9 % rms. An L falls only 20 dB/decade.
  2. 3-level, same L: the voltage step is halved, the cluster drops to about 0.5 % (ripple ≈ 0.9 %). That is the “smaller filter” of the extra level: same ripple with about half the inductance.
  3. LCL (same L1 + L2, plus 10 µF, 1 % reactive power): 60 dB/decade above fres ≈ 3.3 kHz; the fsw cluster falls well below the line. But the undamped peak amplifies whatever sits near fres: on 3-level the largest harmonic is now at 3.3 kHz, not at fsw. Grid background harmonics and the current loop excite the same peak.
  4. LCL + Rd (1.5 Ω): the peak is gone, at the cost of about 6 W (3-level) or 20 W (2-level) in Rd. The alternative is active damping in the current controller.

Design rules (Liserre 2005): 10 f1 < fres < fsw/2; Cf reactive power ≤ 5 % of rating; total L small enough for the voltage drop (≈ 0.1 pu). Passive damping starts from Rd ≈ 1/(3ωresCf).

IEEE 519 limits stop at h = 50; the 0.3 % line is only a visual reference for the kHz range.

Where multilevel converters live today

1500 V PV & storage

Balancing at low power factor and in overmodulation; capacitor and arm-energy sizing; fault tolerance using redundant states; EMI of fast multilevel edges.

Summary

Three things to remember

1 · The modulator makes two decisions. Which level (carriers, with min-max for 15 % more voltage: the space-vector modulator in carrier form), then which state for that level.

2 · Balancing is a modulator job. The offset (NPC), the state choice (flying capacitor), sorting (MMC). Its authority follows the active power or the current sign, so check both power directions.

3 · To the controller, any leg is a voltage source behind a filter. More levels mean smaller steps, so a smaller filter for the same ripple; an LCL needs its resonance damped.
